# Narrative Design — Working Draft

Legal note up front (see [remaster-plan.md](remaster-plan.md)): same story
*beats* as the original — friendly employer, vanished miners, cryptic
transmissions, a horrifying truth at the bottom — but entirely new names,
entirely new written text. Nothing below reuses original dialogue.

## The technique

Fallout's vaults work because you never get a briefing. You get a terminal
log with two lines redacted, a skeleton in a lab coat next to an
overturned chair, and a form letter that's cheerful in exactly the wrong
way. The player assembles the story themselves, and because they built it,
it's theirs — and because no single source is trustworthy, they're never
quite sure they've got it right. That uncertainty is the horror, not a
monster.

Concretely, that means three rules for every piece of narrative content in
this game:

1. **No source is fully reliable.** The employer is corporate-cheerful in a
   way that curdles once you know more. Other miners are dead, panicking,
   or already changed by whatever's down there — they're not lying exactly,
   but they don't have the full picture either, and neither do you.
2. **Every clue raises a question it doesn't answer.** A recovered log
   mentions "the procedure" like it's routine paperwork. A wrecked pod's
   cargo manifest lists something that isn't a mineral. Never follow a
   reveal with an explanation in the same beat.
3. **Physical evidence and spoken/written testimony should occasionally
   contradict each other.** The corp's official incident report says a pod
   was recalled for maintenance. You find that same pod, fused into the
   rock, clearly destroyed in place, months before the report's date.

## The three narrative channels

The original had one channel (radio transmissions on a depth trigger). This
design adds two more, all discovered by the player at their own pace rather
than pushed at them:

**1. Transmissions** (the original's channel, kept, content rewritten) —
short, timed to depth like the original's cadence, auto-played, can't be
replayed on demand (mirrors the original's disposable, half-caught-signal
feel).

**2. Wreckage** — derelict mining pods physically embedded in the terrain
at specific depths, discoverable by digging into them (not marked on any
map — you find them or you don't). Each one is a small, self-contained
story: a stenciled call sign, cargo still in the hold, a shattered black
box you can drill out and play back. This is where "someone else was here
and something happened to them" becomes *physical* rather than just a
voice — you're literally standing where it happened.

**3. Corporate documents** — found as collectible terminal-log-style text
(bulletin fragments, safety memos, revision-stamped internal notices)
scattered underground and at derelict sites. This is the channel for
institutional-horror texture: bland, procedural language describing
something that is very obviously not fine. Sparse — a handful across the
whole game, not a collectathon.

## Setting: the planet Venice

**Venice is the planet**, not the colony — resolves the earlier open
question (Mars vs. a fictional world) in favor of a fictional world, named
in classic sci-fi tradition after an Earth city, not literally set on Earth.
Chosen for a real thematic payoff: the real Venice is famous for slowly,
inevitably **sinking** — threading directly into the title, into "Sunk
Cost" as a design register even though that isn't the final title, and into
the whole descent structure. A whole world sold to colonists/investors on a
name evoking luxury, water, and romance, and it turned out to be a barren,
lethal dig site, is exactly the kind of bait-and-switch irony the corporate
signage below is built to carry.

The colony/outpost itself doesn't need its own separate proper name — it
reads better as purely functional (referred to in dialogue and signage
as "the site," "the facility," a numbered designation) precisely because
nobody who built it thought it would need to be memorable. "Venice" was the
marketing department's word, sold to people who'd never set foot there;
the outpost was always just infrastructure.

## Surface — set the tone before the first dig

Per your note — this needs to read as abandoned *before* the player does
anything:

- A skyline of derelict prefab structures, half-buried in drifted dust —
  clearly built for a much larger operation than "one contractor and four
  shops." A weathered arrival gate or landing-pad sign reading **WELCOME TO
  VENICE** (or a corrupted fragment of it, half the letters dark) is the
  first thing the player sees on emerging from the lab — the planet hasn't
  earned that name in a long time.
- Billboards, powered but flickering, looping cheerful recruitment slogans
  that read wrong in hindsight ("YOUR FUTURE IS **BELOW**." "DEEPER
  QUOTAS, DEEPER REWARDS." "VENICE: WHERE YOUR FUTURE FLOWS." — corporate
  enthusiasm about depth and water imagery on a dry, dead world, which only
  becomes unsettling once the player has a reason to wonder why)
- A boneyard of older digging machines around the outpost's edge, clearly
  failed/abandoned, various states of cannibalization — this is the
  "everyone else is gone" fact stated in silent environmental terms before
  a single transmission plays
- No other people, ever, anywhere on the surface. The only voice is the
  employer's. That absence is itself the first clue.

## Locked direction: what's actually down there

Decided, superseding the earlier three-option branch. Synthesis of RoboCop
(identity/body ownership horror), SOMA (the horror is a question, not a
monster), and *Doctor Who* "Gridlock" (bureaucratic abandonment, fake
progress, isolated people finding comfort in each other over a shared
channel). No literal demon — this reading is decisively sci-fi/institutional,
which formally retires that option.

**Who the player is.** Not a new hire. A catastrophically injured worker,
rebuilt into the mining pod itself, redeployed under a standard reactivation
procedure that treats every redeployment like onboarding day whether or not
the "hire" is new. The player doesn't know this at first — it's the whole
opening (below).

**Who the Foreman is.** One AI, two registers. The clinical, checklist voice
from the wake-up sequence and the warm, cheerful, depth-bonus-dangling
"employer" voice from the surface loop are the *same entity* — not two
characters. It isn't lying so much as running two very different scripts
without reconciling them, because nothing has updated it in a long time.

**Why "mine, mine, mine."** The AI's operational directive has degraded
over time down to a single surviving clause. Extraction was never the whole
original mission — just the one instruction that stayed intact. A redacted
document found late in the depths can show a fragment of a much longer
original directive list without ever completing it.

**Why the surface is empty.** Not a mystery to solve so much as a fact to
notice: the colony above hasn't had staff in a long time. Nobody's
listening to the Foreman's reports. It keeps running the operation anyway —
Gridlock's abandoned-motorway logic exactly. This lands harder for having
been set up visually from the very first surface shot (see Surface section
above), rather than sprung as a twist.

**Why the wreckage is full of other pods.** Not equipment failures — other
reactivated units, other redeployments, sent out before the player and
never returned. Retroactively recontextualizes every wreckage beat in the
depth sheet above without needing new content there.

**Why other transmissions sometimes aren't distress calls.** Some of the
other voices heard on the way down are, like the player, converted workers,
isolated in their own shafts but able to hear each other over a shared
frequency — checking in, occasionally just talking to not be alone. This is
the Gridlock hymn-channel beat: warmth and solidarity threaded through the
horror, not just more dread. Keeps the tone from being uniformly bleak.

**The single dream beat.** One short, unrepeated hallucinatory flash —
pre-tank memory, a fragment of the life before the accident (a face, a
name), placed around the -4,500ft "motherload" tone-shift or immediately
before the final descent. Deliberately not a mechanic, not a loop, not
replayed — a single beat, cheap to build, high impact. Complements rather
than duplicates the opening (opening = post-wake disorientation; this =
pre-accident memory).

**The ending.** Not a boss fight against a villain — a confrontation at
whatever remains of the original facility/mission, framed as much
personal-stakes (how much of "you" is left, what you do about it) as
survival-horror climax. Exact staging still open; happy to draft once the
rest of the script direction feels settled.

## The opening — the hook

Cold open, first-person, no HUD yet:

1. **Wake-up inside the tank/pod itself.** Muffled audio, fluid draining,
   restraints releasing. The pod's exterior lights flicker on before any
   dialogue plays, so the player registers "I am the machine" visually, in
   passing, before anyone explains anything. Nothing announces the reveal.
2. **The room.** Rows of specimen tanks, most shattered or drained. A few
   still intact, contents unidentifiable in murk — species deliberately
   never resolved. Skeletal remains near dead consoles, some humanoid, some
   not quite. Decades of visible neglect. No exposition text needed — the
   art carries this entirely.
3. **First AI line, mid-sentence, procedural.** Addresses the player by a
   unit designation, not a name. Doesn't comfort or explain — resumes a
   checklist as if no time passed. This doubles as the control tutorial:
   "test propulsion," "test drill," phrased as operational calibration, not
   a how-to-play prompt. The horror beat and the tutorial beat are the same
   beat.
4. **Optional, missable environmental details** on the way out — a cracked
   photo, a faded placard, a terminal fragment. First taste of the
   corporate-document channel, entirely skippable.
5. **Surface.** Doors open on the outpost from the Surface section above.
   Cut to the Foreman's *other* register — cheerful, standard onboarding —
   totally disconnected from what the player just saw. Nobody in-fiction
   acknowledges the wrongness. The game then plays like an ordinary mining
   loop, and the player carries the question the game will never directly
   answer.

Keep this short — a few minutes, not a cutscene-heavy prologue. Mostly
environmental art and a handful of terse AI lines; doesn't need a dialogue
budget to land.

## What stays permanently unexplained

Per the core technique above, none of these are ever confirmed, by design:
- What the original experiment/mission was actually for
- How long the player was in the tank
- Whether the unit numbering implies few or many prior subjects
- Whether the player is still meaningfully human
- Whether the Foreman "knows" what it's doing or genuinely can't perceive
  the contradiction anymore
